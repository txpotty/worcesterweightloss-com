    Semaglutide vs. Tirzepatide: How the Two Leading GLP-1 Medications Compare

    Oakwell MD care team · October 15, 2026 · 6 min read

    Semaglutide and tirzepatide are the two names that come up first in almost every weight loss consult. Semaglutide is the active ingredient in Wegovy and Ozempic, while tirzepatide is the active ingredient in Zepbound and Mounjaro. Each one is a single shot under the skin, once a week. They act on gut hormones that tell the brain you have eaten enough, yet they differ in how they are dosed and how the stomach tends to react.

    How each medication works

    Semaglutide copies GLP-1, a hormone your gut releases after a meal. GLP-1 slows stomach emptying, signals fullness to the brain and helps the pancreas release insulin when blood sugar rises, and the drug version lasts about seven days in the body instead of a few minutes. That is why it is weekly. One pen, one day, every week.

    Tirzepatide activates the GLP-1 receptor plus a second one called GIP, another gut hormone involved in insulin release and how the body stores energy. That second target matters. Hitting both receptors seems to add to the appetite effect in trials. Patients on either drug often describe less food noise, the steady hum of thoughts about food that quiets down within the first few weeks for many people.

    What the clinical trials showed

    In the STEP 1 trial, adults without diabetes taking semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly lost about 15 percent of their body weight on average over 68 weeks, compared with roughly 2 percent on placebo. In SURMOUNT-1, tirzepatide at the top dose of 15 mg produced an average loss of about 21 percent over 72 weeks. The SURMOUNT-5 study later compared the two head to head, and tirzepatide came out ahead on average. Averages hide a lot. Some people lose far more than the mean and some lose less, so neither number predicts your result.

    Dosing, titration and side effects

    Both drugs start low and climb slowly. Wegovy begins at 0.25 mg weekly and steps up every four weeks to a 2.4 mg maintenance dose, so most people spend about 16 weeks reaching the full dose. Zepbound starts at 2.5 mg and rises in 2.5 mg steps every four weeks, up to a maximum of 15 mg. Staying on a lower step for an extra month is common and perfectly reasonable.

    Nausea is the most common complaint with either medication, followed by diarrhea and constipation. In the trials, these effects were usually mild to moderate and tended to show up after a dose increase, then fade over a week or two as the body adjusted. Some patients tolerate one drug better than the other. A switch between them is a reasonable option if one sits poorly.

    Neither drug is for everyone. The labels carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rodents, so they are not used in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2. They are also avoided in pregnancy, and a history of pancreatitis or gallbladder disease gets a closer look from the provider.

    How a provider helps you choose

    The choice is usually practical. Expect questions about your blood sugar history and anything you take now. Pharmacy supply and insurance coverage also play a part, and both can change from one month to the next without much warning.

    • Tirzepatide had the higher average weight loss in head-to-head data.
    • Semaglutide has the longer track record, including a cardiovascular outcomes trial.
    • Semaglutide also comes in a daily tablet form, which some patients prefer.
    • Side effect patterns are similar, but tolerance differs from person to person.

    Switching between the two is common. A patient who stalls on semaglutide 2.4 mg, or who cannot tolerate it, may move to tirzepatide, usually starting at 2.5 mg or 5 mg depending on the provider's judgment rather than at a matched dose. Expect a fresh titration. Some stomach upset may return briefly after changing drugs.

    Weight is not the only outcome providers weigh. Semaglutide lowered the risk of heart attack and stroke by about 20 percent in the SELECT trial of adults with heart disease and overweight. Zepbound is also approved for obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. For some patients, that tips the decision.

    Ozempic and Mounjaro are FDA-approved for type 2 diabetes, not weight loss, even though they contain the same ingredients as Wegovy and Zepbound. Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide are not FDA-approved products, and a provider prescribes them only when clinically appropriate. Ask which one you are getting. The label on the vial or pen box tells you.
