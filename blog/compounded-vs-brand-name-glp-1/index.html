    Compounded vs. Brand-Name GLP-1s: What Patients Should Know

    Oakwell MD care team · September 27, 2026 · 6 min read

    Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide became widely known during the national shortages of 2022 through 2024, when pharmacies were allowed to make copies of drugs on the FDA shortage list. Both shortages have since been declared resolved, which changed the rules for many compounders. Compounding still exists. Here is a plain explanation of what that means for patients.

    What brand-name means

    Wegovy and Ozempic (semaglutide) and Zepbound and Mounjaro (tirzepatide) are made by their manufacturers and approved by the FDA. Wegovy and Zepbound are approved for chronic weight management, and Zepbound is also approved for moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. Ozempic and Mounjaro are approved for type 2 diabetes. Weight loss use of those two is off-label. Each pen is made under the same manufacturing controls, batch after batch.

    Insurance coverage for brand drugs varies a lot by plan. Many employer plans cover Wegovy or Zepbound only with prior authorization, which usually requires a documented BMI and sometimes proof of a prior diet attempt. Medicare rules on weight loss coverage have been changing. Manufacturer savings programs and direct-to-patient pharmacies exist for people paying cash, and the details change often.

    What compounded means

    A compounded medication is prepared by a licensed pharmacy for an individual patient based on a prescription. They are not FDA-approved, so no FDA review of that exact product's safety or quality happened before it was dispensed. 503A pharmacies compound for individual prescriptions under state board oversight, while 503B outsourcing facilities register with the FDA and follow federal manufacturing standards. Ask which kind your pharmacy is.

    Each 503A pharmacy makes each prescription for a named patient. A 503B facility can produce larger batches and must follow current good manufacturing practice, the same quality rules drug makers follow. Both can be legitimate. The difference is mostly in testing and oversight.

    Since the shortages ended, federal rules limit compounders from making what are essentially copies of an approved drug. Prescribers usually need a patient-specific reason, for example a dose strength the brand pen does not come in or a sensitivity to an inactive ingredient. Some compounded products add vitamin B12 or glycine. Those additions have not been shown to improve weight loss.

    Real differences patients notice

    • Form: brand drugs come in prefilled pens, while compounded versions usually come in a vial you draw up with a syringe.
    • Dosing units: compounded vials are labeled in mg per mL, so your dose may be written in units on an insulin syringe.
    • Storage: most compounded vials need refrigeration and have a beyond-use date printed on the label.
    • Coverage: insurance may cover brand drugs for some patients, and compounded products are rarely covered.

    The dosing math is where errors happen. A vial of 2.5 mg per mL means 0.25 mg is 10 units on a 100-unit insulin syringe, and mixing up milliliters and units can lead to a tenfold overdose. The FDA has received reports of exactly that. Have your provider or pharmacist walk you through the first draw.

    Storage rules differ too. Brand pens can usually sit at room temperature for a limited time once in use, but a compounded vial generally lives in the fridge, between 36 and 46 degrees Fahrenheit. Check the beyond-use date on the label, and do not use the vial past it even if liquid remains. Cloudy liquid or floating particles mean you should call the pharmacy.

    How to judge a compounded source

    Quality varies between pharmacies. The FDA has warned about counterfeit semaglutide and products sold online labeled for research use only, which are not meant for people. A legitimate prescription goes through a licensed provider and a state-licensed pharmacy that you can look up on your state board of pharmacy website. Salt forms such as semaglutide sodium or acetate are not the same as the base drug, and the FDA has said it is not aware of a lawful basis for compounding with them.

    Red flags are easy to spot once you know them. Be wary of any seller that skips the medical questionnaire, ships without a prescription, or will not name the pharmacy. The label on a real prescription shows your name, your prescriber, the strength in mg per mL and a beyond-use date. Missing any of those is a reason to stop.
